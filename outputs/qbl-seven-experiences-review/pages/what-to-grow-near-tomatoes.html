    The short answer

    A small, useful
    support crew.

    Try basil, onions, early lettuce, carrots nearby, and flowers at the edges. Build around the tomatoes first. Give them support, airflow and picking room; fit the companions around that space.

    These are practical ways to share a bed—not a promise that a companion plant will prevent pests or improve every harvest.

    01 / Choose the company

    Good neighbors.
    Different jobs.

    Start with the plants you’ll use. Then think about their size, timing and place in the bed.

    The kitchen partner

    Basil

    Useful close by, without turning the tomato patch into a wall of herbs. Tuck it near a few plants and leave the tomatoes their space.

    See the basil growing guide ↗
    The narrow neighbor

    Onions

    A side row or a few lower pockets put another edible in the bed without a bulky second canopy.

    Explore onions ↗
    The opening act

    Lettuce, early

    Use a front strip early in the season. As the tomato canopy fills in, let the lettuce finish rather than leaving it buried.

    Explore lettuce ↗
    A little space of their own

    Carrots, nearby

    A mixed bed can make room for carrots. Nearby is the useful idea—not packed directly under every tomato.

    Explore carrots ↗
    Keep the work zone open

    Flowers at the edges

    Let flowers brighten the corners and outside margins. Keep them out of the space you need for supporting and picking tomatoes.

    Why this works as a layout

    Think space.
    Think season.
    Then companions.

    1. Keep some neighbors low.Onions stay narrow. Basil can be useful nearby without filling every gap.
    2. Let an early crop finish.Lettuce has a different role from a plant that stays through the whole tomato season.
    3. Use the margins.Flowers belong where they add interest without taking over the picking and support space.
    What companion planting does—and doesn’t—promise

    A companion list is a starting point. The useful idea is to share space while keeping access, timing and the size of the plants in mind. It is not a guarantee of pest control or a bigger harvest.

    For the evidence behind particular plant relationships, see University of Minnesota Extension’s companion-planting guide.

    Keep the layout simple.

    1. Place the tomatoes first. Leave room for their supports, growth and harvest access.
    2. Choose a little company. Basil near a few plants; onions in a side row or lower pockets.
    3. Give carrots their own pocket. Don’t fill every tomato base with another crop.
    4. Keep the front flexible. Early lettuce finishes. Flowers stay at the corners and outer edge.

    Leave a little breathing room

    A full bed.
    Not a tangled one.

    The usual mistake is trying to make every free inch do another job.

    01

    Don’t crowd the canopy.

    Dense growth makes the bed harder to work in. Keep support and picking room in the plan.

    02

    Don’t let the base stay messy.

    Watch crowded lower growth and lingering wet foliage. Airflow matters.

    03

    Let the filler finish.

    Early lettuce should not spend the summer struggling under the tomato vines.

    03 / Protect your tomatoes

    Something chewing?
    Look closer first.

    Missing leaves, curled growth and damaged fruit are clues. They are not a diagnosis on their own.

    Look → identify → act

    Start with the plant,
    not the spray bottle.

    Check stems, leaf undersides, tender growth and developing fruit. Look for insects, droppings and sticky residue as well as the damage itself.

    During pest season, look over the plants every day or two. Catch a change while it is still small, then identify what is causing it.

    Open the garden-help finder↗
    01 / Notice

    Leaves disappearing

    What you might see: Large missing chunks of foliage or dark droppings.

    Look along stems and under leaves for large green caterpillars. Hornworms are one possibility; confirm the insect before acting.

    02 / Notice

    Tender growth changing

    What you might see: Curled new growth, sticky residue or clusters of tiny insects.

    Look closely at the undersides and new shoots. Aphids are one possibility; curling alone does not identify them.

    03 / Notice

    Other clues in the patch

    What you might see: Small holes, insects scattering when disturbed, fruit damage, or a young transplant cut at soil level.

    The source also discusses flea beetles, whiteflies, stink bugs and cutworms. Compare the actual insect and damage before choosing a response.

    After identification: practical first steps

    For confirmed hornworms, the source recommends hand-picking. For confirmed aphids, it recommends dislodging them with water and checking again. Keep tomato plants supported and avoid letting dense lower growth become a damp tangle.

    For other treatment choices, start with a confirmed identification and crop-specific guidance. “Natural” is not a substitute for knowing what the problem is.

    Compare hornworm identification with UMN Extension or use its tomato leaf-damage diagnostic guide.
