Grow Extra Herbs for Yourself, Friends, and a Couple Extra Dollars

A practical backyard guide to what extra herbs actually turn into, how people really use or move the overflow, and how a small herb setup can create kitchen value, gift value, and maybe a little extra money too.

This is not about turning your backyard into a farm stand.

It is about growing herbs in a way that gives you more than one kind of payoff. Fresh use. Drying. Gifts. Starts. Maybe a few small sales if you end up with more than you need.

Most of the value comes from herbs you were already happy to grow anyway.

What extra herbs actually turn into

The basil problem

You plant a few basil plants for yourself, then suddenly you have more than you can use fresh.

Use some.

Freeze some.

Root some cuttings.

Example: 12 rooted starts × $3 = $36

The mint monster

One mint plant can give you tea, garnish, drying herbs, and gift bundles before the season is over.

Example: 10 tea bundles × $4 = $40

The oregano drying rack

Oregano is one of the easiest herbs to turn into pantry value because drying works so well.

Example: 8 to 10 small spice jars = about $24 to $30 in value

Dividing chives

Chives are not flashy, but they are one of the easiest herbs to divide, gift, or trade later.

Example: 3 extra divisions × $4 = $12

Backyard value ladder

Level 1, use it yourself

Save money by replacing the herbs you would have bought anyway.

Level 2, give extras away

Herb bundles, starts, and jars of dried herbs make easy neighbor gifts.

Level 3, trade extras

Plants, eggs, produce, and other backyard swaps all count as real value.

Level 4, sell occasional extras

Think small real backyard money, not business fantasy money.

What I would actually grow

4 basil plants

For kitchen use, pesto, rooted starts, and quick fresh overflow.

1 mint container

For tea, drinks, drying, and gift bundles.

2 oregano plants

For drying and long pantry value.

1 thyme plant and 1 chive clump

For steady kitchen use, drying, and future divisions.

Potential extra value: about $50 to $200 in a season, without turning the yard into a production farm.

The biggest value usually is not selling

A basil plant that keeps you from buying basil all summer has already done something useful.

The occasional rooted start, tea bundle, dried jar, or divided clump is usually extra value on top of that, not the main point.
