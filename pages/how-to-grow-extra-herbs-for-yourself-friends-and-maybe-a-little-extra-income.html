Grow Extra Herbs for Yourself, Friends, and a Little Extra Income

Real backyard costs, real harvests, and real-world value. An honest look at what a few common herbs can produce over a season if you grow them well and actually use the harvest.

Most herbs do not look like much when you buy them.

A $4 basil plant. A $5 mint plant. A small pot of thyme.

They fit in one hand.

A few months later, many of those same herbs can produce far more food, seasoning, tea, starter plants, or dried herbs than most people expect.

This is not a business plan. It is simply a look at how much value a small herb purchase can realistically create in an ordinary backyard.

The ground rules

For potted herbs

  • herb plant: about $5
  • pot: about $5
  • potting mix: about $3
  • water or fertilizer share: about $2

Total startup: about $15

For in-ground herbs

  • starter plant: about $5
  • soil or compost share: about $3
  • water or fertilizer share: about $1 to $2

Total startup: about $8 to $10

The point is not perfect accounting. The point is comparing one herb to another in a realistic backyard way.

What extra herbs actually turn into

1. The basil problem

You planted 3 basil plants. By mid July, you cannot use it all fast enough.

  • use what you want fresh
  • freeze pesto
  • give some away
  • root a tray of cuttings

Example: 12 rooted basil starts × $3 = $36

That is not life-changing, but it can pay for seed, soil, and still leave plenty for your kitchen.

2. The mint monster

You planted 1 mint plant. By August, it is trying to take over the pot.

  • fresh tea
  • summer drinks
  • drying
  • small gift bundles

Example: 10 tea bundles × $4 = $40

That is from the plant that was already getting too big anyway.

3. Dividing chives

You start with one mature clump. Next spring, you split it.

  • keep one clump
  • gift one
  • sell or trade the rest

Example: 3 extra divisions × $4 = $12

Not exciting. But it is repeatable, and that is part of what makes chives useful.

4. The oregano drying rack

One productive oregano plant can quietly fill a lot of little jars.

  • dry the harvest
  • fill your own spice jars first
  • gift or trade the extra

Example: 8 to 10 small spice jars at about $3 each = $24 to $30

Or keep them and skip buying oregano for a long time.

Backyard value ladder

Level 1

Use it yourself.

Save about $20 to $100 by replacing things you would have bought anyway.

Level 2

Give extras away.

Build goodwill with herb bundles, starts, or a jar of dried oregano.

Level 3

Trade extras.

Plants, eggs, produce, or other small backyard swaps all count as real value.

Level 4

Sell occasional extras.

Think $20 to $200 per season, not quit-your-job herb money.

What I would actually grow

4 basil plants

For kitchen use, pesto, rooted starts, and quick fresh overflow.

1 mint container

For tea, drinks, drying, and gift bundles, without letting it run wild.

2 oregano plants

For drying and long pantry value.

1 thyme plant and 1 chive clump

For steady kitchen use, drying, and future divisions.

Potential extra value: about $50 to $200 in a season, without turning the backyard into a production farm.

The most important thing

The biggest value usually is not selling anything.

It is replacing purchases.

A basil plant that keeps you from buying basil all summer has already paid for itself. The occasional starter plant, tea bundle, dried herb jar, or divided clump is simply extra value on top.

Bottom line

A $5 herb plant probably will not change your life. But a handful of productive herbs can easily produce $50 to $200 worth of food, seasoning, tea, starter plants, and garden value over a season.

For something that fits in a small pot, that is a surprisingly good return.
