How to Grow Extra Herbs for Yourself, Friends, and Maybe a Little Extra Income

A practical backyard guide to the herbs people most often grow, dry, bundle, replant, gift, and sometimes sell in small quantities, with ballpark seed costs and national-average value ranges.

Why herbs keep showing up in grow-for-profit advice

  • small space can still produce useful volume
  • many herbs can be sold or used in more than one format
  • culinary herbs are easier to explain than niche herbs
  • starter cost is low compared with many bigger crops

What this page is really about

  • kitchen savings first
  • better use of companion herbs you are already growing
  • extra starts, bundles, dried herbs, or tea herbs second
  • small realistic backyard value, not fantasy farm math
Important note: the numbers below are ballpark national-average ranges pulled from common backyard growing, retail herb, and small-scale herb-selling patterns. They are not promises of what you will make in your town.

What people on the internet are actually doing with these herbs

Fresh kitchen packs

Most beginner herb sellers start with familiar culinary herbs like basil, parsley, cilantro, dill, and chives because the use is obvious and the buyer does not need much explanation.

Dried herbs

Thyme, oregano, mint, chamomile, rosemary, and lavender keep showing up because drying stretches value and reduces the pressure to sell everything immediately.

Starter plants

Small herb starts are one of the easiest backyard formats because people understand them fast and the price point is simple.

Gift and neighbor formats

Potted herbs, tied bundles, little tea herbs, and divided clumps keep coming up because they feel personal, practical, and low-pressure.

How to plant, grow, dry, and use them in the most practical ways

Best herbs for fresh packs

  • basil
  • parsley
  • cilantro
  • dill
  • chives

Grow these in repeat sowings or multiple clumps so you can cut small amounts steadily instead of one big all-at-once harvest.

Best herbs for drying

  • thyme
  • oregano
  • mint
  • lavender
  • chamomile

Cut before they get ragged, bundle lightly, and hang in a dry airy spot out of harsh sun.

Best herbs for starter plants

  • basil
  • chives
  • mint
  • oregano
  • thyme

These are some of the easiest to picture as little potted starts, divisions, or rooted extras.

Best herbs for tea or bundles

  • mint
  • chamomile
  • lavender
  • thyme in blends

These are the herbs most often turned into simple tea bundles, calming blends, or giftable dried packs.

What usually makes the most business sense first

1. Replace your own grocery buys

Start by growing the herbs you actually buy or wish you bought more often. This is the cleanest first win.

2. Test a second format

If basil is growing well, try a few extra starter plants. If thyme is doing well, dry some. If mint is taking off, make tea bundles.

3. Stay with familiar herbs

Most small growers get better results starting with herbs people already recognize and use.

4. Let demand tell you what grows next

Pay attention to what friends, neighbors, and local buyers actually ask for instead of guessing too far ahead.

Bottom line: the strongest herb strategy for a backyard grower is usually not “find the rarest herb.” It is “grow the most useful herbs in formats people already understand, and let the same square foot produce kitchen value, garden value, and maybe a little extra value beyond that.”
