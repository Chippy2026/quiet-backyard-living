How to Grow Oregano: The Herb That Keeps Paying You Back

A practical backyard guide to growing oregano for cooking, drying, and years of harvests.

Quick answer: if basil is the sprinter, oregano is the marathon runner. A single oregano plant can provide years of harvests, dries exceptionally well, survives neglect better than many herbs, and keeps producing long after flashier herbs are gone.

Why oregano is one of the smartest herbs you can grow

Most herbs get judged by how much they produce and how good they taste. Oregano adds a third category: longevity.

Plant it once. Harvest for years. That is where much of its value comes from.

What oregano likes

Sun

Oregano loves full sun.

  • 6+ hours daily
  • more sun usually means stronger flavor
  • more sun usually means better drying quality too

Well-drained soil

Oregano prefers average, sandy, or even rocky soil more than soggy, overly rich soil.

Airflow

Open spacing and breezy spots help with flavor, drying quality, and disease prevention.

What oregano hates

  • constantly wet roots
  • poor drainage
  • deep shade
  • overwatering
  • heavy clay

Watering oregano

One of oregano’s biggest strengths is that it does not need constant attention.

Once established, water deeply, then let the soil dry somewhat before watering again.

Think Mediterranean herb, not water garden plant.

Seed vs starter plant

Seed is inexpensive and gives you lots of plants, but it starts slower.

Starter plants are easier for beginners and get you to the useful part faster.

For most people, start with a plant.

Container vs garden

Containers are excellent because they make harvesting easy, keep spreading controlled, and let you move the plant if needed.

A 10 to 12 inch pot is a good starting point.

In garden beds, oregano often becomes larger, tougher, and more productive over time.

How big does oregano get?

Typical plants often reach 12 to 24 inches tall and 18 to 36 inches wide.

Older oregano plants can become surprisingly large and productive.

The secret to bigger harvests

Do not just pick random leaves. Harvest stems, especially upper growth, before flowering. That encourages branching, thicker plants, and more harvests.

Usually start harvesting when the plant is 6 to 8 inches tall and looks established.

How often can you harvest?

During the growing season, every few weeks is realistic for a healthy plant.

Oregano often rebounds quickly once it is established.

The flower question

Unlike basil, the answer is sometimes yes.

Oregano flowers attract pollinators, look nice, and do not dramatically ruin the plant.

However, best flavor usually comes before flowering.

Best harvest time

For drying, right before flowering is often best.

That is when the oils are strongest and the flavor is usually at its best.

Why oregano is a drying superstar

This is where oregano really separates itself from basil.

Basil loses a lot during drying. Oregano thrives. The flavor concentrates, storage becomes easy, and the value lasts for months.

How to dry oregano

  • harvest stems
  • remove damaged leaves
  • bundle loosely
  • hang upside down
  • dry in shade with airflow

Usually this takes 1 to 2 weeks.

How much can one plant produce?

A mature oregano plant can provide dozens of harvests, multiple drying batches, and enough seasoning for many months.

Equivalent store value can easily land in the $25 to $80+ range depending on the plant and how you use it.

What to do with extra oregano

Fresh cooking

Great in pizza, pasta sauce, roasted vegetables, and soups.

Dry it

This is usually the best option.

Gift it

Think herb jars, seasoning blends, or kitchen baskets.

Trade it

Dried oregano stores well and is easy to share.

Can you divide oregano?

Yes, and it is one of the coolest parts of growing oregano.

After a few years, you can dig up the plant, split it into sections, and replant. One plant becomes several.

Winter survival

In many climates, oregano comes back every year.

Winter may kill top growth, but the roots survive. Spring arrives, and oregano returns again.

When is oregano finished?

Annual basil dies. Oregano often does not.

Many gardeners get 3 years, 5 years, or even longer from one plant.

Common oregano problems

Weak flavor

Usually not enough sun.

Yellow leaves

Usually too much water.

Sparse growth

Usually needs pruning.

Woody center

Older plants get woody. Cut back hard or divide.

Oregano timeline

Spring: New growth
Early summer: First harvests
Mid summer: Best drying harvest
Late summer: More harvests
Fall: Final cuttings
Winter: Dormant
Spring again: Returns
Why oregano scores so well in the Herb Hub: one $5 oregano plant can become fresh meals, dried spice jars, gifts, new divisions, and years of harvests. Very few herbs check all five boxes.
Bottom line: if basil is the herb that gives you the biggest payoff this season, oregano might be the herb that gives you the biggest payoff over several seasons. Plant it once. Harvest it often. Dry it well. And let it quietly earn its place in the backyard year after year.
