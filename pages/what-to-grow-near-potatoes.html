What to Grow Near Potatoes

Potatoes do best when the bed stays practical and not too crowded, with companions that do not create too much root competition or shade.

Good nearby friends for potatoes

  • Beans nearby A classic useful partner if spacing is handled well.
  • Cabbage family crops nearby Often mentioned as reasonable neighbors in mixed gardens.
  • Marigolds Easy diversity and color nearby.
  • Horseradish at an edge Sometimes used traditionally if someone has room for it.

Did you know?

  • Potatoes are one of those crops that can feel simple up top but do a lot underground.
  • Hilling matters more than many people expect.
  • Crowding the root zone can make a potato bed more frustrating fast.

A simple potatoes bed example

A simple beginner-friendly mix could look like this:

  • potatoes as the main crop
  • marigolds nearby
  • beans with enough room
  • cabbage family crop in a nearby section

What to be careful about

Potatoes need room underground as much as above.

  • Do not let shading plants crowd them too hard.
  • Watch drainage so they are not sitting wet too long.

Protect my potatoes, what actually helps?

If your potato plants are getting chewed up, the first pest to suspect is usually the Colorado potato beetle. It is one of the most common real potato headaches, and it can go from a few striped beetles to a serious leaf-eating problem faster than people expect.

  • What to watch for Adult beetles have yellow-orange shells with dark stripes. The soft red or orange larvae look a little blob-like and can strip leaves fast. Also check the undersides of leaves for clusters of bright orange eggs.
  • What naturally helps most Hand-picking beetles and larvae still works surprisingly well in a backyard patch. Knock them into a container of soapy water, crush egg clusters when you find them, and stay on it early before the population builds.
  • Use row cover early If you know beetles are a regular problem in your area, lightweight row cover early in the season can help protect young plants before beetles settle in. Just remember flowering is less of an issue with potatoes than some crops, but the cover still needs to come off for airflow and easier checking once plants get bigger.
  • Rotate where potatoes go Do not keep planting potatoes in the same exact spot year after year if beetles or disease pressure has been bad. Rotation will not magically solve everything, but it can make the bed less predictable for pests.
  • Mulch and cleanup help too A tidy bed, less plant stress, and fewer hiding places around badly infested debris can all help. Mulch is still useful for moisture and soil health, but do not let old problem foliage just sit there if it is covered in pests.
  • Check often, not just once The biggest mistake is waiting too long. A two-minute leaf check every day or two is usually much easier than dealing with a full blow-up later.

Best first move

Start by checking the undersides of leaves for orange egg clusters and look for striped adults near the top of the plant.

Good natural potato pest routine

  • check leaves every day or two during pest season
  • remove beetles, larvae, and egg clusters early
  • keep the plants watered and not stressed
  • rotate the crop when possible
  • do not let a small outbreak become the new normal

What not to do

  • Do not ignore the first few beetles.
  • Do not assume one spray solves the whole problem.
  • Do not leave badly infested leaves in place forever if they are packed with larvae or eggs.

A few more potatoes basics that really matter

  • Soil pH: potatoes often do well around 5.0 to 6.5
  • Loose soil: helps tubers form better
  • Drainage: important so tubers are not sitting soggy
  • Hilling: helps protect and build around developing tubers
  • Sun: full sun usually helps a lot

Bottom line

If you like potatoes, keep the bed practical, give the crop what it actually needs, and add just a couple helpful neighbors instead of chasing a perfect chart.

And if potato beetles show up, do not panic. Catch them early, stay consistent, and use simple old-school pressure relief before the whole patch gets away from you.
