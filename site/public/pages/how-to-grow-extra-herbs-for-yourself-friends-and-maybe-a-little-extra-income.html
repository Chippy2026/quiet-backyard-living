How to Grow Extra Herbs for Yourself, Friends, and Maybe a Little Extra Income

If you are already growing herbs for companion planting or kitchen use, this is how to think about them as a practical backyard value crop with real uses, real savings, and a few realistic ways they might pay for themselves.

Herbs are one of the few backyard crops that can realistically do several jobs at once. They can help nearby vegetables, give you repeated kitchen harvests, dry well for later, and sometimes become small starter plants, bundles, or extras people actually want.

This is not about pretending a few basil plants are a major business. It is about recognizing that herbs often have a better value-to-space ratio than many bigger garden crops.

The practical business case

A single herb plant can support a vegetable bed, give repeated harvests, reduce small grocery purchases, and still leave room for extra starts, dried herbs, or shareable bundles. That is a better stack of uses than many ordinary backyard crops can offer.

Why herbs are such a good place to start

  • they fit small spaces better than many vegetables
  • you often use them in small amounts, but repeatedly
  • store herb pricing can feel high relative to how little you get
  • some are easy to dry, divide, pot up, or replant
  • some help nearby vegetables too
  • they are easier to experiment with than many bigger crops

What the numbers usually look like in real life

Fresh herbs are small, but repeated

A small basil or parsley bunch can easily sell for around $2 to $4 worth of value at grocery-store scale, even though the seed cost was only a few dollars.

Dried herbs can stretch the value further

Thyme and oregano often become more valuable after drying because a little fresh growth can turn into something that lasts for months.

Starter plants may be the best beginner “extra”

A small herb starter plant often feels more tangible to people than loose herbs, and can carry $4 to $6 value from a plant that did not cost much to begin.

Space efficiency matters

Herbs often earn more value per square foot than many bulk vegetables because you use them repeatedly and waste less of the harvest.

The best herbs to grow a little extra of

Basil

Fast, useful, and one of the easiest herbs to imagine as a fresh bunch, a kitchen herb plant, or an extra crop near tomatoes and peppers. Seed cost is low, and repeat cuts come quickly.

Parsley

Not flashy, but very practical. That matters because familiar herbs are easier to use, easier to share, and easier for other people to understand and want.

Thyme

Small, fragrant, dryable, and one of the best examples of a little plant going a long way. A modest plant can produce a surprising amount of usable dried herb over time.

Oregano

One of the best herbs for drying and storing, which is part of what makes it such a good long-game value crop. It is simple, familiar, and easy to turn into lasting kitchen value.

Chives

Easy to divide, easy to use, and one of the friendliest herbs for little garden clumps and starter plants. They are one of the easiest herbs to multiply without much drama.

Mint

Very productive for tea and drinks, but best kept in pots unless you want mint becoming the boss of the bed. For the right person, it is one of the strongest volume herbs you can grow.

What usually makes the most sense first

Best realistic entry points

  • fresh kitchen herbs you would have bought anyway
  • dried herbs for your own pantry first
  • extra starter plants or divisions from herbs that multiply well
  • small bunches or potted extras for friends and neighbors

How to grow extra without making the whole garden feel like a job

Start with herbs you already use

If you do not use it much, it is harder to notice the value and harder to feel excited about growing more of it.

Grow one or two extra plants first

You do not need a field of basil. A few extra plants can already give you more kitchen use, more drying value, and something shareable.

Keep harvest gentle and steady

Frequent little harvests often work better than hacking a plant down all at once.

Pay attention to what people respond to

Sometimes the best clues come from what friends, neighbors, or family naturally ask about first.

Simple ways herbs might pay for themselves

  • fresh bunches for your own kitchen instead of buying them
  • dried herbs for later use
  • a few extra starter plants or divisions
  • tea herbs in small bundles
  • little potted herbs for friends or neighbors
  • gifts that still come from something useful you were already growing

In plain terms, this is about reducing small repeated purchases first, then noticing whether certain herbs also create a little extra value beyond that.

Keep this grounded

You do not have to pretend herbs are a huge money machine. The smarter business mindset is that they are one of the easiest backyard categories to test because the startup cost is low, the use is familiar, and the downside risk is small if a few experiments go nowhere.

If I were starting this lane from scratch

  • grow basil, parsley, thyme, oregano, and chives first
  • plant some near vegetables where they make sense
  • grow a little extra of the ones you actually use
  • dry some before the season ends
  • divide or pot up extras later if certain herbs do especially well

Bottom line

If you are already growing herbs for companion planting or kitchen use, growing a little extra is one of the simplest ways to get more value out of the same backyard space. The numbers are small, but the math is friendly: low startup cost, repeated use, good space efficiency, and several possible ways for the same herb to pay you back.
