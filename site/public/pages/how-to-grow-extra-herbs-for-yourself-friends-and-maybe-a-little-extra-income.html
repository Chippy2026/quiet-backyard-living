How to Grow Extra Herbs for Yourself, Friends, and Maybe a Little Extra Income

If you are already growing herbs for companion planting or kitchen use, this is how to think a little bigger without making it weird, pushy, or overcomplicated.

Herbs are one of the easiest places to start if you want your backyard to do a little more. They can help a bed feel more balanced, give you something fresh to use in the kitchen, dry well for later, and sometimes turn into a little extra value beyond that.

You do not need to build a full business around them. Sometimes the smartest move is just growing enough to use well, share a little, and see what people naturally respond to.

The real goal

Grow herbs that earn their space more than once, in the garden, in the kitchen, and maybe later as starter plants, dried bundles, or little extras for people nearby.

Why herbs are such a good place to start

  • they fit small spaces better than many vegetables
  • you often use them in small amounts, but repeatedly
  • some are easy to dry, divide, or replant
  • some help nearby vegetables too
  • they feel familiar and useful to other people
  • they are easier to experiment with than many bigger crops

The best herbs to grow a little extra of

Basil

Fast, useful, and one of the easiest herbs to imagine as a fresh bunch, a little kitchen herb plant, or a “grow extra” crop near tomatoes and peppers.

Parsley

Not flashy, but very practical. That makes it easy to use yourself and easy for other people to understand too.

Thyme

Small, fragrant, dryable, and one of the best examples of a little plant going a long way.

Oregano

One of the best herbs for drying and storing, which is part of what makes it such a good long-game value crop.

Chives

Easy to divide, easy to use, and one of the friendliest herbs for little garden clumps and starter plants.

Mint

Very productive for tea and drinks, but best kept in pots unless you want mint becoming the boss of the bed.

How to grow extra without making the whole garden feel like a job

Start with herbs you already use

If you do not use it much, it is harder to notice the value and harder to feel excited about growing more of it.

Grow one or two extra plants first

You do not need a field of basil. A few extra plants can already give you more kitchen use, more drying value, and something shareable.

Keep harvest gentle and steady

Frequent little harvests often work better than hacking a plant down all at once.

Pay attention to what people respond to

Sometimes the best clues come from what friends, neighbors, or family naturally ask about first.

Simple ways herbs might pay for themselves

  • fresh bunches for your own kitchen instead of buying them
  • dried herbs for later use
  • a few extra starter plants or divisions
  • tea herbs in small bundles
  • little potted herbs for friends or neighbors
  • gifts that still come from something useful you were already growing

Keep this grounded

You do not have to pretend herbs are a huge money machine. The smarter mindset is that they are one of the easiest categories to save a little money with, enjoy a lot, and maybe stretch into something extra later if it feels natural.

If I were starting this lane from scratch

  • grow basil, parsley, thyme, oregano, and chives first
  • plant some near vegetables where they make sense
  • grow a little extra of the ones you actually use
  • dry some before the season ends
  • divide or pot up extras later if certain herbs do especially well
